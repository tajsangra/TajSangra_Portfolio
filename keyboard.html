Project Overview

Idea

I had never worked with PCB's before and wanted a project that would force me to learn the process from schematic to fabrication. I chose a keyboard as you can vary the difficulty of the project as you progress by adding more features and because I needed a new keyboard.

Schematic Design

I learned how keyboard matrices work by starting with a 2x2 matrix then moving to a full row and eventually a full keyboard. After this I added per key and underglow RGB, a coloured OLED screen and a rotary encoder but, I had run out of GPIO pins for the OLED so I had to remake the matrix from 7x15 to 9x10.

PCB Design

I assigned footprints to every part but I had to make the OLED and rotary encoder footprints from scratch. Then I designed the layout of the keyboard and moved all the parts accordingly. Finally, I routed everything, added mounting holes and created a ground copper pour.

Plate Design

I first created the switch openings directly in KiCad, but then rebuilt the plate using an open-source plate generator so I could use the correct H-shaped switch cutouts. Because my layout was custom, I generated each row separately and aligned them manually in KiCad before adding the OLED opening and mounting geometry.

CAD Design

I designed the enclosure in SolidWorks around the PCB and plate, adding screw bosses and mounting points for the top and bottom case. The case dimensions and top cover were iterated several times, and the final model included dedicated mounting bosses and clearance for the OLED.

Assembly & Soldering

I hand-soldered the capacitors, RGB LEDs, Kailh hot-swap sockets, controller, and supporting hardware. Assembly also required rework: one LED row was installed backwards, four copper pads were damaged during correction, and I repaired the affected connections with jumper wires.

Hardware & PCB Design

The uploaded KiCad PCB contains 84 mechanical switch footprints, per-switch diodes, 92 reverse-mount SK6812MINI-E LEDs, 0.1 µF decoupling capacitors, larger bulk capacitors for power smoothing, a Raspberry Pi Pico footprint, OLED and rotary-encoder provisions, a 74AHCT125 RGB logic-level controller, an RGB data-line resistor, and six M2 mounting holes.

Debugging

Debugging

Debugging and Rework

The first problems appeared before fabrication. KiCad reported schematic issues because many global labels were visually placed on wires but were not actually snapped to the wire endpoints. Those labels had to be moved and the schematic rechecked before the design was considered clean.

The largest design failure came after the first PCB was ordered: every Kailh hot-swap socket had been flipped to the wrong side. The footprint already accounted for backside mounting, so flipping the footprint again put the sockets on the wrong side and made the first PCB unusable. I corrected all socket orientations, rerouted the board, and ordered a second revision.

Assembly created another failure mode. One row of RGB LEDs was soldered backwards, and while reorienting them I tore four copper pads from the PCB. The damaged LED connections were recovered with hand-soldered jumper wires, which are visible on the back of the finished board.

The OLED and plate also required mechanical rework. The OLED PCB could not fit through the original plate opening, so I enlarged the cutout with a Dremel. I then mounted the display using spacers to hold it at the correct height, washers to support the screw heads over the larger OLED mounting holes, and M1.2 screws to secure the assembly.

NEXT REVISION

Future Improvements

01

Increase RGB brightness

The main improvement would be powering the RGB LEDs from the dedicated 5 V power rail instead of sourcing their power from a GPIO pin, which would provide more available current and allow the lighting to reach a higher, more consistent brightness. I would also use lower-resistance power paths and additional 5 V/GND power-injection points across the RGB chain to reduce voltage drop while keeping the RGB data signal controlled through the logic-level circuitry.

02

Use a higher-quality case material

The 3D-printed enclosure works for the prototype, but manufacturing the case from aluminum or another more durable material would improve stiffness, weight, surface finish, and long-term durability while giving the final keyboard a more finished feel.

03

Increase the switch-opening tolerance

The switch insert holes in the plate are tighter than necessary, which makes installing and removing switches more difficult, so I would make the cutouts slightly larger while still maintaining enough retention to hold each switch securely in the plate.

04

Correct the OLED plate cutout

The OLED opening in the plate was undersized, so I had to enlarge it manually with a Dremel during assembly. In a future revision I would dimension the cutout around the full OLED PCB and its mounting geometry before fabrication so the display fits correctly without any post-processing.
